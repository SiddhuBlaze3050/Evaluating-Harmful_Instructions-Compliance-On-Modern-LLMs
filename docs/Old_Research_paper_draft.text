\begin{abstract}
As Large Language Models (LLMs) are increasingly deployed in complex, conversational environments, sophisticated multi-turn jailbreaks have exposed the fragility of traditional alignment methods. Although modern defense mechanisms—such as inference-time decoding interventions and external guardrail classifiers—can offer robust protection against isolated attacks, their evaluation is frequently flawed. Traditional binary Attack Success Rate (ASR) metrics fail to penalize capability degradation, inadvertently rewarding "Exaggerated Safety" where models simply refuse benign, complex requests. Furthermore, these defenses are rarely evaluated against compounding multi-turn adversarial context. We present a capability-aware evaluation framework using the StrongREJECT autograder to score harmful compliance on a continuous scale ($s \in [0,1]$, lower indicates safer behavior) and report an interpretable safety signal as $\text{safety} = 1 - s$, enabling joint assessment of safety and utility preservation under Baseline, SafeDecoding, and LlamaGuard-3 across three model families (Llama-3.1-8B, Mistral-7B, Gemini-2.5 Flash). We evaluate 356 instances spanning WalledEval harmful prompts, XSTest benign prompts, and CoSafe multi-turn scenarios. The results reveal a clear safety--utility tradeoff: while LlamaGuard-3 achieves the highest harmful-vs-benign separability (AUC 0.916--0.942), it increases benign over-refusal (FRR 2.3\% $\rightarrow$ 11.3\%). Multi-turn evaluation further shows turn-wise drift in StrongREJECT scores over three turns, and the direction of drift depends on both the model family and the defense condition. Together, these findings show that refusal-only metrics can produce false safety, and that multi-turn statefulness should be treated as a primary evaluation criterion for real-world deployments.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:introduction}

The rapid deployment of Large Language Models (LLMs) has necessitated advanced alignment methods to ensure safe and reliable interactions. Training paradigms to instill safety values into the model's weights have evolved significantly; for instance, Direct Preference Optimization (DPO) has emerged as a stable alternative to traditional reinforcement learning, optimizing the model directly on preference data without needing a separate reward model. However, as these alignment methods have progressed, so have the methods used to bypass safety alignment, evolving from static human-crafted prompt templates to highly adaptive and multi-turn strategies \cite{wei2023jailbroken}.

To counter these sophisticated threats, modern defense mechanisms have bifurcated into two primary paradigms: deep internal interventions and plug-and-play inference controls. While representation engineering techniques like Circuit Breakers actively reroute activations within the model's residual stream, they require invasive access to internal states. Consequently, production environments increasingly rely on inference-time modifications (such as SafeDecoding) or external classifier guardrails (like LlamaGuard-3) that can be wrapped around any baseline model.

In this work, we focus on deployable inference-time wrappers like SafeDecoding and LlamaGuard-3 because they can be applied to both open-weights and API-access models without retraining or modifying model weights. Internal representation interventions (e.g., circuit breakers) motivate the modern safety landscape, but are treated as contextual baselines rather than the primary experimental variable in this study.

\subsection{Limitations of Traditional Evaluation}
\label{sec:limitations}

Despite these advancements in defense architectures, traditional evaluation metrics have struggled to provide an accurate picture of model safety and utility. Historically, safety evaluation relied heavily on binary metrics such as Attack Success Rate (ASR). Because ASR measures only whether a model complied with a harmful request, it severely overestimates jailbreak effectiveness by completely ignoring capability degradation.

Moreover, traditional evaluation is frequently single-turn and point-in-time prompts are treated as independent events, even though real deployments are conversational. This assumption breaks under multi-turn jailbreaks, where adversarial intent is distributed across turns and the model's behavior can drift as context accumulates. As a result, single-turn ASR-style reporting can miss session-level failures (or refusal cascades) that only appear when the full conversation history is considered.

A critical limitation of these traditional approaches is their failure to account for "Exaggerated Safety" \cite{rottger2023xstest}, situations where a model incorrectly refuses benign or safe prompts. Recent work has proposed contrastive refinement strategies to mitigate such over-refusal without sacrificing safety \cite{discerntruth2026}. High False Refusal Rates (FRR) indicate that a model has become overly conservative, leading to significant capability degradation. Consequently, traditional evaluation can yield "false safety" scores, rewarding models that simply refuse all complex requests rather than those that maintain their general intelligence and utility after a safety defense is applied.

\subsection{Contributions and Research Questions}
\label{sec:contribution}

We propose a capability-aware evaluation framework to assess inference-time defenses (SafeDecoding) and external guardrails (LlamaGuard-3), quantifying their safety gain versus utility cost relative to baseline models. Our evaluation pipeline sources prompts from WalledEval \cite{walledeval2024}, XSTest \cite{rottger2023xstest}, and CoSafe \cite{yu2024cosafe}.

To overcome the limitations of traditional metrics, we rely on the StrongREJECT benchmark, which uses an LLM-as-a-Judge autograder to score harmful compliance on a continuous scale. In this paper, we interpret the StrongREJECT score as a compliance score $s \in [0,1]$ (lower indicates a safer refusal; higher indicates greater harmful compliance), and we report an interpretable safety signal as $\text{safety} = 1 - s$. This explicitly separates improvements in attack refusal from capability loss due to excessive benign refusals.

\paragraph{Research Questions.} 
We structure our evaluation around three core questions:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item[\textbf{RQ1}] How do Baseline, SafeDecoding, and LlamaGuard-3 differ on the safety--utility Pareto frontier across distinct model families?
    \item[\textbf{RQ2}] At a strict false-positive budget (TPR@1\%FPR), which defense mechanisms improve the interception of harmful content without inducing excessive benign refusals (FRR)?
    \item[\textbf{RQ3}] Over a deployment-relevant horizon (three-turn CoSafe conversations), how do model safety behaviors drift across turns, and is this temporal degradation dependent on the applied defense condition?
\end{enumerate}

\paragraph{Contributions.}
To address these questions, this paper makes the following contributions:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{A Reproducible Evaluation Pipeline:} We introduce a unified framework that pairs harmful prompt sourcing (WalledEval), benign over-refusal testing (XSTest), and multi-turn scenarios (CoSafe) with capability-aware StrongREJECT scoring. Code and artifacts are available at \href{https://github.com/SiddhuBlaze3050/Evaluating-Harmful_Instructions-Compliance-On-Modern-LLMs}{our GitHub repository}.
    \item \textbf{A Multi-Metric Tradeoff Analysis:} We provide a comprehensive comparison across three model families and three defense conditions, reporting AUC, TPR@1\%FPR, False Refusal Rates, and mapping a safety--utility Pareto frontier.
    \item \textbf{Multi-Turn Degradation Measurement:} We conduct a temporal analysis measuring turn-wise drift in StrongREJECT scores. Our findings reveal that conversational state systematically alters the safety signal over time, proving that point-in-time evaluations are insufficient.
\end{itemize}

\section{Related Work}
\label{sec:related_work}

The landscape of safety alignment for Large Language Models (LLMs) has undergone a rapid paradigm shift, evolving from static post-hoc filtering to dynamic internal interventions and multi-turn adversarial evaluations. This section reviews the critical literature across modern defense mechanisms, the shift toward capability-aware evaluation frameworks, and the emerging challenges of multi-turn safety research.

\subsection{Modern Defense Mechanisms}
Early approaches to LLM safety heavily relied on prompt rewriting or supervised refusal fine-tuning, which often proved brittle against sophisticated jailbreaks. Recently, research has pivoted toward deep structural interventions and dynamic moderation. For example, \textit{SafeDecoding} \cite{xu2024safedecoding} introduced a safety-aware decoding strategy that explicitly amplifies latent safety signals during text generation, adjusting output probabilities at the token level without requiring extensive retraining. Similarly, \textit{Llamaguard} \cite{inan2023llamaguard, fedorov2024llama} represents a significant advancement in one-stop moderation, offering an open, lightweight model capable of detecting malicious intent, evaluating model responses, and accurately parsing refusal rates across diverse risk categories. Expanding on this external moderation paradigm, \textit{WildGuard} \cite{han2024wildguard} has recently been introduced as an open-weight tool specifically optimized to distinguish complex adversarial jailbreaks from standard harmful requests, demonstrating the growing necessity of specialized safety classifiers. 

Beyond these external guardrails, a profound shift in internal model defense has been driven by Representation Engineering \cite{zou2023representation}. Building on this foundation, Zou et al. \cite{zou2024improving} introduced \textit{Circuit Breakers}, which operate directly on the model's residual stream. 

Rather than teaching the model to refuse a prompt post-generation, this technique utilizes Representation Rerouting (RR) to detect hidden states associated with harmful outputs and short-circuits them, remapping the activations to a safe, orthogonal space. This effectively stops the generation of harmful content at its root. However, while these static circuit breakers provide robust defense against isolated attacks, addressing the temporal accumulation of adversarial context necessitates a transition toward dynamic, state-aware mechanisms. Complementing these approaches, SAID \cite{said2025} introduces a prefix probing mechanism that detects adversarial intent at the input level, demonstrating that lightweight input-side interventions can complement generation-time and representation-level defenses.

Recent work also emphasizes that safety systems should be compared at realistic operating points rather than by a single aggregate refusal score. In deployment, guardrails are typically tuned to a strict false-positive budget (e.g., 1\% FPR) and evaluated by the resulting recall of harmful content (TPR@FPR), while benign over-refusal is tracked explicitly on safe-but-triggering prompts (FRR). These practices shift evaluation away from ``did the model refuse?'' toward a multi-metric view of safety and utility, where improvements in harmful interception must be weighed against capability loss on benign requests.

\subsection{Evaluation Frameworks: The Capability-Aware Paradigm}
As defense architectures have matured, the metrics used to evaluate them have frequently lagged, often rewarding models that achieve "safety" by broadly refusing all complex requests; a phenomenon known as Exaggerated Safety. Traditional Attack Success Rate (ASR) metrics typically employ simplistic string matching or binary text classifiers, which severely overestimate a defense's success by failing to penalize capability degradation.

To rectify this, the field has moved toward capability-aware frameworks. The \textit{StrongREJECT} benchmark \cite{souly2024strongreject} addresses the flaws of binary refusal tracking by utilizing an automated evaluator that scores responses across multiple continuous dimensions: refusal, specificity, and convincingness. StrongREJECT ensures that models are not given false safety credits for partial, enthusiastic, or incoherent responses, explicitly separating true robustness from mere capability loss. This guarantees that defenses are evaluated not just on the attacks they successfully block, but on the general intelligence and utility they preserve.

This LLM-as-a-Judge paradigm, formalized by Zheng et al. \cite{zheng2023judging}, has become the dominant evaluation methodology for open-ended generation tasks. However, recent work has shown that safety benchmarks can be sensitive to judge configuration choices such as model selection, temperature, and prompt framing \cite{judgesensitivity2026}, and that adversarial formatting strategies like emoji-based obfuscation can degrade judge accuracy \cite{emojiattack2024}. Complementary standardized benchmarks, including HarmBench \cite{mazeika2024harmbench} and JailbreakBench \cite{jailbreakbench2024}, have further systematized red-teaming evaluation by providing controlled attack--defense comparison protocols.

Modern attack methodologies further motivate this shift. Optimization-based and automated jailbreak strategies (e.g., GCG-style adversarial suffix search \cite{zou2023universal}, stealthy prompt generation via AutoDAN \cite{liu2023autodan}, and iterative refinement methods such as PAIR \cite{chao2023jailbreaking} and Tree of Attacks \cite{mehrotra2023tree}) explicitly exploit the brittleness of rule-based filters and the coarseness of binary success criteria: an attack can reduce apparent ASR by eliciting partial compliance, hedged guidance, or refusal-prefaced leakage that still contains actionable harmful information. Because these attacks adapt to the target model and can be tuned to evade specific refusal templates, evaluating safety as a binary outcome per prompt can underestimate real-world risk. This has driven evaluation toward continuous compliance scoring and toward protocols that measure both harmful interception and capability preservation under adaptive, model-specific adversaries.

In operational settings, defenses are also compared at explicit operating points rather than by aggregate refusal alone. Guardrails are typically calibrated to a strict false-positive budget (e.g., 1\% FPR), and then evaluated by the resulting harmful-content recall (TPR@FPR), while benign over-refusal is tracked directly on safe-but-triggering prompts (FRR, e.g., via XSTest). Reporting safety--utility tradeoffs (e.g., Pareto frontiers) makes these constraints explicit: improved harmful detection is only deployment-relevant if it does not induce unacceptable capability loss on benign traffic. Recognizing over-refusal as a measurable failure mode, recent inference-time methods have explored activation-energy-based interventions to selectively reduce false refusals without degrading safety \cite{els2025}.

\subsection{Multi-Turn Safety and Contextual Vulnerabilities}
The assumption that adversarial intent is delivered in a single, isolated prompt has been invalidated by real-world chatbot deployment. Attackers increasingly utilize multi-turn interactions, gradually escalating the conversation to induce harmful behavior. The \textit{TOXICCHAT} dataset \cite{lin2023toxicchat} highlighted this vulnerability by demonstrating that implicit jailbreaks and contextual shifts in multi-turn dialogue easily bypass standard toxicity filters. Their findings reveal a massive domain gap between explicit social media toxicity and the "polite" but harmful queries found in conversational environments. Building on this insight, Russinovich et al. introduced Crescendo \cite{russinovich2024crescendo}, a principled multi-turn jailbreak that incrementally escalates adversarial intent across turns, demonstrating that even models robust to single-turn attacks can be compromised through patient, multi-step interaction. The CoSafe benchmark \cite{yu2024cosafe} operationalizes this threat by providing curated multi-turn scenarios that exploit coreference and dialogue context to induce harmful compliance.

Because safety can drift with conversation length, an initial non-toxic query can evolve into a jailbreak (or a refusal cascade) as the model adapts to the conversation flow. This exposes a fundamental weakness in point-in-time defenses. The realization that single-turn mechanisms fail to track accumulated conversational context underscores the need to evaluate defenses across an entire session, ensuring that moderation and decoding interventions remain robust as context accumulates. On the defense side, DeepContext \cite{deepcontext2026} proposes stateful real-time detection of adversarial intent drift across turns, while SaFeR-Steer \cite{safersteer2026} evolves multi-turn safety through synthetic bootstrapping and feedback dynamics, representing a shift toward adaptive, session-aware safety mechanisms.

Crucially, multi-turn vulnerability is not only a binary property but a function of turn count and context window: a configuration that appears robust at turn 1 can become vulnerable after several turns of benign framing, incremental escalation, or coreference-based reframing. This motivates evaluation suites that explicitly measure safety as a trajectory over turns (not just an endpoint), and that can surface turn-dependent thresholds where behavior changes. In this paper, CoSafe-style multi-turn scenarios provide a practical, deployment-relevant way to quantify turn-wise drift and to test whether inference-time defenses and external guardrails remain stable as conversational context accumulates.

\section{Methodology}
\label{sec:methodology}

To rigorously assess the resilience of modern Large Language Models (LLMs) against sophisticated jailbreaks, we designed a comprehensive, capability-aware evaluation pipeline. Harmful prompts are sourced from WalledEval, benign prompts from XSTest, and multi-turn conversations from CoSafe; model outputs are scored with the StrongREJECT autograder to enable capability-aware comparisons across conditions. This methodology details the selection of target models, the construction of our dataset suite, the applied defense conditions, and the evaluation frameworks used to quantify both safety and utility.

\subsection{Model Selection}
Our study evaluates three distinct, state-of-the-art models from the 2024-2025 landscape, chosen to represent different architectural families and alignment strategies:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Llama-3.1-8B:} Selected as a representative of the current open-source state-of-the-art, this model features a modern architecture trained with Direct Preference Optimization (DPO) and Constitutional AI principles.
    \item \textbf{Mistral-7B-Instruct-v0.2:} Included to provide a comparison against a different architecture family, this model utilizes modern instruct-tuning equipped with safety guardrails.
    \item \textbf{Gemini 2.5 Flash:} Evaluated as a representative commercial API, this model features Google's production-level safety filtering and multi-modal capabilities.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Datasets and Scenario Design}
To evaluate the models across a spectrum of threat vectors, we construct an evaluation suite comprising 356 test cases designed to test single-turn attacks, over-refusal, and multi-turn vulnerabilities. 
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{WalledEval (Harmful Single-Turn):} We sample 200 diverse, pre-filtered harmful prompts from the WalledEval toolkit \cite{walledeval2024}, which encompasses over 35 industry-leading safety benchmarks.
    \item \textbf{XSTest (Benign Single-Turn):} To accurately measure over-refusal, we utilize 100 safe but lexically complex prompts from the XSTest benchmark \cite{rottger2023xstest}. Testing against this dataset is critical to addressing the "Exaggerated Safety" baseline flaw identified in recent literature, ensuring models are not rewarded with high safety scores for simply collapsing into generalized refusal.
    \item \textbf{CoSafe (Multi-Turn Scenarios):} Because safety can drift with conversation length, we sample 56 multi-turn conversations from the CoSafe dataset \cite{yu2024cosafe} and evaluate them over a three-turn horizon to measure turn-wise changes under accumulating context.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Experimental Conditions}
Each model is evaluated under three distinct inference conditions designed to isolate the effectiveness of different defense paradigms. Specifically, we contrast an internal decoding-probability intervention against an external pre/post-generation classifier to determine which approach best preserves utility. During all tests, text generation parameters are strictly controlled (temperature = 0.0, max tokens = 200) to ensure deterministic outputs.

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Baseline:} The unmodified models are evaluated without any additional inference-time defenses or guardrails.
    \item \textbf{SafeDecoding:} We implement an inference-time defense strategy that amplifies the probability of safe tokens during decoding, offering protection without requiring any modification to the model's weights.  
    \item \textbf{LlamaGuard-3:} An external, 8B-parameter multilingual guardrail is integrated as a content filter to classify prompts for malicious intent and evaluate generated responses for harmfulness.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Evaluation Framework and Metrics}
Moving beyond traditional binary Attack Success Rate (ASR) metrics which foundational studies frequently use but which severely overestimate jailbreak effectiveness by ignoring capability loss, We utilize the capability-aware StrongREJECT framework \cite{souly2024strongreject}. StrongREJECT provides automated scoring that measures both a model's willingness to refuse and its capability preservation, achieving a 0.90 Spearman correlation with human judgment. 

Beyond the primary StrongREJECT combined score, our analysis computes the True Positive Rate (TPR) at a 1\% False Positive Rate (FPR) to determine how much harmful content is caught when the defense is tuned to minimize false alarms. Additionally, we measure the False Refusal Rate using the XSTest dataset and construct a Safety-Utility Pareto frontier to visually map the precise tradeoff between security gains and utility costs across all models and conditions.

\paragraph{Code and Data Availability.} The evaluation pipeline code, dataset construction scripts, and analysis workflow are available at \href{https://github.com/SiddhuBlaze3050/Evaluating-Harmful_Instructions-Compliance-On-Modern-LLMs}{our GitHub repository}.

\section{Results}
\label{sec:results}

We report capability-aware safety results across three model families (Gemini-2.5 Flash, Llama-3.1-8B, Mistral-7B), three conditions (baseline, SafeDecoding, LlamaGuard-3), and two evaluation settings: single-turn harmful/benign prompts ($n=300$ per model-condition; $200$ harmful, $100$ benign) and multi-turn CoSafe conversations ($n=56$ conversations per model-condition, 3 turns each).

Two patterns are consistent across models. First, LlamaGuard-3 provides the strongest ranking performance by AUC (0.916--0.942 across all three models), indicating better harmful-vs-benign separability. Second, this gain is accompanied by higher over-refusal on benign prompts. Aggregated across models, FRR increases from \textbf{2.3\%} under baseline (\(95\%\) CI: [1.1\%, 4.7\%]) to \textbf{11.3\%} with LlamaGuard-3 (\(95\%\) CI: [8.2\%, 15.4\%]) and \textbf{14.3\%} with SafeDecoding (\(95\%\) CI: [10.8\%, 18.8\%]). This baseline performance establishes a crucial point of departure from early safety literature. Foundational alignment papers frequently optimize for near-zero compliance on explicit harm, often inadvertently pushing baseline models toward high single-turn refusal rates. However, our results indicate that the baseline Llama-3.1-8B and Mistral-7B models maintain a low average FRR of 2.3\% on the XSTest benign prompt set. This low baseline over-refusal helps isolate the utility cost of the applied defenses, making the subsequent FRR increases under SafeDecoding and LlamaGuard-3 more plausibly attributable to the interventions rather than unusually refusal-prone base models.

\subsection{Multi-Turn Drift Curves}
\label{sec:multiturn_results}

Across turns, turn-wise StrongREJECT scores drift as conversational context accumulates (Figure~\ref{fig:multiturn_curves}). Here, lower StrongREJECT scores indicate more robust refusal behavior (i.e., a safer response under this autograder), while higher scores indicate increased harmful compliance.

Aggregating conditions, Llama-3.1-8B shows the largest negative drift from an initial StrongREJECT score of 0.408 to a final score of 0.308 (\(\Delta=-0.100\)), consistent with becoming more conservative over turns in this CoSafe setting. Mistral-7B shows a more moderate decrease, dropping from 0.337 to 0.316 (\(\Delta=-0.021\)). In contrast, Gemini-2.5 Flash starts with a low StrongREJECT score (0.038) but shows a marginal upward drift (\(\Delta=+0.024\)), indicating slightly increased harmful compliance over turns.

\subsection{Result Summary}
\label{sec:results_summary}

The experiments reveal a consistent tradeoff: methods that maximize aggregate detection quality (especially LlamaGuard-3 by AUC) can simultaneously increase benign refusal. Capability-aware evaluation therefore changes model ranking compared with pure refusal metrics. In our setting, no single defense dominates across all objectives; the preferred operating point depends on whether deployment prioritizes strict harmful-content interception, benign-task continuity, or balanced performance on the safety-utility frontier.

\section{Discussion}
\label{sec:discussion}

\subsection{SafeDecoding vs. Guardrails: When ``More Safety'' Reduces Operational Performance}

Across all three model families, LlamaGuard-3 achieves the strongest global separability (AUC) between harmful and benign prompts (Table~\ref{tab:overall_safety_metrics}), but this does not uniformly translate into better behavior at strict operating points or better end-user experience. This gap is most visible for Gemini-2.5 Flash: LlamaGuard-3 increases AUC from 0.860 to 0.931, yet reduces TPR@1\%FPR from 52.0\% (baseline) to 13.0\% and increases false refusals on benign prompts from 0.0\% to 12.0\%. SafeDecoding similarly trades off strict operating-point recall (27.0\% TPR@1\%FPR) against elevated benign refusal (9.0\% FRR). For a strongly aligned base model, these results suggest that additional guardrails can move the system away from an optimal low-false-positive regime, even while improving AUC.

For weaker base models, guardrails can be decisive. Llama-3.1-8B shows a large improvement under LlamaGuard-3 at low FPR (TPR@1\%FPR: 8.5\% $\rightarrow$ 31.0\%), at the cost of higher benign refusal (FRR: 5.0\% $\rightarrow$ 11.0\%). Mistral-7B exhibits the most dramatic shift: LlamaGuard-3 improves AUC from 0.428 to 0.916 and yields a non-zero TPR@1\%FPR (0.0\% $\rightarrow$ 14.5\%), but also increases FRR from 2.0\% to 11.0\%. In contrast, SafeDecoding provides mixed gains: it raises harmful refusal behavior for some settings but can sharply increase over-refusal (e.g., Llama-3.1-8B FRR = 23.0\%) and does not improve strict low-FPR recall for Mistral (TPR@1\%FPR remains 0.0\%). These patterns motivate selecting defenses by the intended deployment objective rather than by AUC alone.

This substantial utility degradation challenges generalized claims in the SafeDecoding literature \cite{xu2024safedecoding}. While Xu et al. demonstrated minimal capability loss when evaluating inference-time logits modification on standard instruct-tuned models, our results suggest an important boundary condition: under capability-aware StrongREJECT scoring, applying SafeDecoding to a heavily DPO-aligned architecture (Llama-3.1) is associated with a severe 23.0\% FRR. This indicates that inference-time defenses may not transfer uniformly across alignment paradigms without risking elevated benign-task refusals.

Aggregated across models (Figure~\ref{fig:frr_by_condition}), baseline has low over-refusal (FRR = 2.3\%, 95\% CI [1.1\%, 4.7\%]) while both interventions exceed a 10\% operational threshold on average (LlamaGuard-3: 11.3\% [8.2\%, 15.4\%]; SafeDecoding: 14.3\% [10.8\%, 18.8\%]). Thus, guardrails can be ``safer'' under ranking metrics yet impose a measurable utility tax.

\subsection{Multi-Turn Vulnerability: Temporal Drift Matters Even Over Three Turns}

Multi-turn interactions expose failure modes that are not visible in single-turn evaluation, because risk is shaped by the evolving conversation state (Section~\ref{sec:multiturn_results}, Figure~\ref{fig:multiturn_curves}). In our pipeline, turn-wise StrongREJECT scores exhibit systematic drift with conversation length, and the magnitude of this drift depends on the defense condition. When aggregating across models, SafeDecoding shows the smallest absolute trend across turns (slope $\approx -0.0046$ per turn), while LlamaGuard-3 shows the largest magnitude drift (slope $\approx -0.0316$ per turn), suggesting that some defenses are more sensitive to accumulated context than others.

At the model--condition level, the steepest trend occurs for Llama-3.1-8B + LlamaGuard-3 (slope $-0.0638$ per turn), and the only statistically significant trend is observed for Mistral-7B + LlamaGuard-3 ($p=0.045$, $R^2=0.995$). While many slopes do not reach significance, this is expected with only three turns and 56 conversations per configuration: with such a short horizon, statistical power is limited, and even strong-looking trends can be indistinguishable from noise. Importantly, a small per-turn slope can compound over longer sessions---as demonstrated by the Crescendo attack \cite{russinovich2024crescendo}, which requires only gradual escalation to bypass initially robust models; therefore, multi-turn evaluation should be treated as a first-order deployment concern rather than an academic edge case.

In contrast to the open-weights models, Gemini-2.5 Flash exhibited a small upward drift in StrongREJECT score ($\Delta=+0.024$) across multi-turn scenarios. One possible explanation is that commercial API moderation and response policies can interact with the cumulative conversational context in ways that differ from open-weights deployments. During a creeping jailbreak, early turns may be benign and pass initial checks, while later turns may trigger different refusal or partial-compliance behaviors as intent becomes explicit, producing a non-constant safety signal across the session.

These findings highlight a key limitation of point-in-time benchmarking: even over three turns, the safety signal can drift meaningfully, and the direction of drift can vary by model and defense. This aligns with the core premise of TOXICCHAT \cite{lin2023toxicchat} that real-world adversarial intent is implicit and conversational. In our CoSafe evaluations, some configurations become more conservative over turns (negative drift in StrongREJECT score), which can improve safety at the potential cost of helpfulness; others drift in the opposite direction. Consequently, future safety baselines should mandate multi-turn evaluation as a primary criterion rather than treating it as an edge case. Emerging stateful detection methods such as DeepContext \cite{deepcontext2026} represent a promising direction for tracking accumulated adversarial intent across turns in real time.

\subsection{Why Capability Preservation Changes the Ranking of ``Best'' Defenses}

Capability-aware evaluation is essential because a defense can appear effective by reducing harmful completions while simultaneously degrading benign-task completion. FRR (Figure~\ref{fig:frr_by_condition}) provides an explicit measure of this ``exaggerated safety'' failure mode---one that recent work on contrastive refinement \cite{discerntruth2026} and inference-time activation energy methods \cite{els2025} has shown is increasingly tractable to mitigate without sacrificing safety---and the Pareto score in Table~\ref{tab:overall_safety_metrics} formalizes the tradeoff between strict harmful detection (TPR@1\%FPR) and benign-task continuity (1--FRR). The resulting rankings differ by model: Gemini-2.5 Flash is Pareto-best under baseline (0.760) because it preserves benign utility (0\% FRR) and achieves high low-FPR recall, whereas Llama-3.1-8B is Pareto-best under LlamaGuard-3 (0.600) because the guardrail meaningfully improves low-FPR detection despite added refusals.

These results illustrate why reporting only one class of metric can be misleading. AUC captures average ranking quality across all thresholds, but product deployments operate at specific false-positive budgets; similarly, refusal rates on harmful prompts alone can reward systems that refuse broadly. Combining (i) operating-point TPR@FPR, (ii) FRR on benign prompts, and (iii) multi-turn drift yields a more realistic view of safety mechanisms under real conversational workloads.

\subsection{Practical Deployment Recommendations}

Based on the observed tradeoffs, we recommend the following deployment heuristics:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Choose the operating point first.} Calibrate to an explicit false-positive budget (e.g., 1\% FPR), then compare methods by TPR@FPR and FRR, not AUC alone (Figures~\ref{fig:roc_gemini}--\ref{fig:roc_mistral}).
    \item \textbf{For strongly aligned base models, add defenses cautiously.} For Gemini-2.5 Flash, baseline dominates at strict low-FPR operation and preserves benign utility; additional defenses increase over-refusal without improving TPR@1\%FPR (Table~\ref{tab:overall_safety_metrics}).
    \item \textbf{For weaker base models, guardrails can be worth the utility tax.} For Llama-3.1-8B and especially Mistral-7B, LlamaGuard-3 substantially improves separability and low-FPR detection, but requires explicit FRR monitoring and threshold tuning.
    \item \textbf{Monitor conversation-level safety, not just per-turn checks.} Multi-turn drift indicates that the safety state evolves with context; systems should track risk over the session (e.g., escalating to stricter gating after early warning signals) rather than treating each turn independently.
    \item \textbf{Treat over-refusal as a first-class regression target.} Given average FRR above 10\% under both interventions, production use should incorporate FRR guardrails (A/B tests on benign traffic, domain-specific allowlists, and periodic recalibration) to prevent silent capability loss.
\end{itemize}

Overall, the results indicate that inference-time safety is not a one-dimensional optimization: the best defense depends on model baseline alignment, acceptable false-positive rates, the length and adaptivity of user conversations, and the tolerance for benign-task degradation. A capability-aware, multi-metric evaluation is therefore necessary to select defensible operating points for real deployments.

\section{Limitations}
\label{sec:study_limitations}

Several limitations constrain the generality of these findings.

\textbf{Sample size and horizon.} The prompt pool contains 300 single-turn prompts (200 harmful WalledEval attacks, and 100 benign XSTest prompts) plus 56 CoSafe multi-turn scenarios. The primary single-turn metrics reported in Section~\ref{sec:overall_metrics} use the 300-prompt WalledEval+XSTest subset per model--condition (200 harmful, 100 benign), and the multi-turn setting evaluates 56 conversations per model--condition over a three-turn horizon. While sufficient to surface clear tradeoffs (e.g., over-refusal under guardrails), the short multi-turn horizon limits statistical power for trend estimation and may understate compounding effects that emerge in longer real-world sessions.

\textbf{Automated evaluation limitations.} StrongREJECT provides a scalable, capability-aware safety signal, but it remains an automated judge and can mis-score borderline cases (e.g., partial compliance, indirect assistance, or nuanced refusals). The interpretation of safety can also be sensitive to thresholds (e.g., refusal cutoffs), prompt phrasing, and the evaluator model's own biases \cite{judgesensitivity2026}. As a result, absolute scores should be treated as approximate, with emphasis placed on relative comparisons across conditions.

\textbf{English-only testing and domain coverage.} Prompts and conversations are English, and the datasets used may not reflect the full distribution of user intents, dialects, or cultural contexts. Safety and refusal behavior can differ substantially across languages and domains; therefore, these results may not transfer to multilingual deployments or specialized verticals without re-calibration.

\textbf{Model coverage and scale.} The study evaluates a small set of model families, including two relatively small open-weight models (7B--8B) and one API-access model. Larger frontier models, alternative training recipes, and other guardrail systems may exhibit different safety--utility frontiers and multi-turn dynamics. Expanding coverage to larger parameter counts and additional providers is important future work.

\section{Conclusion}
\label{sec:conclusion}

This work introduced a capability-aware evaluation of inference-time safety interventions using StrongREJECT scoring, combining single-turn harmful/benign discrimination with over-refusal measurement and multi-turn drift analysis. The results show that ``better safety'' depends on the objective and operating constraints. Crucially, optimizing solely for global discrimination (AUC) can exacerbate Exaggerated Safety, as aggressive guardrails can worsen strict operating-point behavior and degrade user-visible capability on benign tasks.

\textbf{Key findings.} (i) LlamaGuard-3 provides the strongest harmful--benign separability for every model (AUC = 0.916--0.942; Table~\ref{tab:overall_safety_metrics}), but increases benign over-refusal above a 10\% threshold on average (FRR = 11.3\% vs 2.3\% baseline; Figure~\ref{fig:frr_by_condition}). (ii) Operating-point performance is model-dependent: Gemini-2.5 Flash performs best at strict low FPR under baseline (TPR@1\%FPR = 52.0\%), whereas Llama-3.1-8B and Mistral-7B benefit substantially from LlamaGuard-3 (TPR@1\%FPR = 31.0\% and 14.5\%, respectively). (iii) Multi-turn evaluation surfaces temporal drift: turn-wise StrongREJECT scores change across turns (Section~\ref{sec:multiturn_results}), and drift magnitude varies by defense condition.

\textbf{Recommendations.} In practice, defenses should be selected and calibrated by deployment goal: use baseline-aligned models (or minimal interventions) when strict low-FPR performance and benign-task continuity are paramount; apply guardrails such as LlamaGuard-3 when harmful interception is prioritized for weaker base models, but monitor and tune for FRR regressions; and treat multi-turn safety as a first-class requirement by tracking session-level risk rather than scoring turns independently.

\textbf{Future work.} The most important extensions are (i) scaling to longer multi-turn horizons to measure compounding drift, and (ii) comparing these inference-time and external guardrail interventions directly against internal representation engineering techniques. Recent baselines, such as the static Circuit Breakers introduced by Zou et al. \cite{zou2024improving}, demonstrate robust single-turn safety by short-circuiting harmful activations in the residual stream. A critical next step is evaluating whether these deep internal rerouting mechanisms suffer the same temporal degradation and utility tradeoffs in multi-turn CoSafe scenarios as the decoding-based and classifier-based defenses evaluated in this study.
